Grade 7 Mathematics Topics

Explore the Grade 7 Mathematics strands and sub-strands.

1. Numbers
  • Whole Numbers

    Whole numbers are 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ... They do not include fractions or decimals.

    Place value: The position of a digit tells its value in a number.

    Example: In 4,582, the digit 5 has a place value of hundreds and a total value of 500.

    Rounding: To round to the nearest hundred, look at the tens digit. If it is 5 or more, round up; otherwise, round down.

    Example: 3,472 rounded to the nearest hundred is 3,500.

  • Factors

    A factor is a whole number that divides another number exactly without leaving a remainder.

    Example: The factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12.

    Divisibility rules: A number is divisible by 2 if its last digit is even; by 5 if it ends in 0 or 5; and by 10 if it ends in 0.

    Prime numbers: A prime number has exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself. Examples are 2, 3, 5 and 7.

    Prime factorisation: Expressing a number as a product of prime numbers. For example, 12 = 2 × 2 × 3.

    HCF: The highest common factor is the greatest factor shared by two or more numbers.

    LCM: The lowest common multiple is the smallest positive multiple shared by two or more numbers.

    Example: HCF of 8 and 12 = 4; LCM of 8 and 12 = 24.

  • Fractions

    A fraction represents part of a whole. It has a numerator (top number) and a denominator (bottom number).

    Example: In 3/5, the numerator is 3 and the denominator is 5.

    Equivalent fractions: Fractions that have the same value. For example, 1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6.

    Simplifying: Divide the numerator and denominator by their highest common factor. For example, 6/8 = 3/4.

    Addition and subtraction: Use a common denominator when the denominators differ. For example, 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4.

    Multiplication: Multiply the numerators together and the denominators together. For example, 2/3 × 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2.

    Division: Multiply by the reciprocal of the second fraction. For example, 1/2 ÷ 1/4 = 1/2 × 4/1 = 2.

    Reciprocal: Swap the numerator and denominator of a non-zero fraction. The reciprocal of 2/3 is 3/2.

  • Decimals

    Decimals represent numbers using a decimal point to separate the whole-number part from the fractional part.

    Place value: The first digit after the decimal point represents tenths, the second hundredths, and the third thousandths.

    Example: In 4.275, 2 represents 2 tenths (0.2), 7 represents 7 hundredths (0.07), and 5 represents 5 thousandths (0.005).

    Multiplication: Multiply as whole numbers first, then place the decimal point according to the total number of decimal places in the factors.

    Example: 1.2 × 0.3 = 0.36.

    Division: When dividing by a decimal, multiply both numbers by the same power of 10 to make the divisor a whole number.

    Example: 4.8 ÷ 0.6 = 48 ÷ 6 = 8.

    Remember: Adding zeros to the right of a terminating decimal does not change its value. For example, 2.5 = 2.50.

  • Squares and Square Roots

    Square of a number: Multiply the number by itself. For example, 5² = 5 × 5 = 25.

    Perfect squares: Numbers obtained by squaring whole numbers. Examples are 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 and 36.

    Square root: A number that, when multiplied by itself, gives the original number. For example, √49 = 7 because 7 × 7 = 49.

    Fractions: Square the numerator and denominator separately. For example, (2/3)² = 4/9.

    Square roots of fractions: Find the square roots of the numerator and denominator when both are perfect squares. For example, √(9/16) = 3/4.

    Decimals: For example, 0.4² = 0.16 and √0.81 = 0.9.

    Remember: Squaring and finding a square root are inverse operations for non-negative numbers.

2. Algebra
  • Algebraic Expressions

    An algebraic expression combines numbers, variables and mathematical operations.

    Variables: Letters such as x, y and n represent numbers that may be unknown or can change.

    Example: "Five more than a number" is written as x + 5.

    Terms: Terms are separated by plus or minus signs. In 3x + 4, the terms are 3x and 4.

    Coefficients: A coefficient is the number multiplying a variable. In 7y, the coefficient is 7.

    Like terms: Terms with the same variables raised to the same powers. For example, 2x + 3x = 5x.

    Simplifying: Collect like terms to make an expression shorter. For example, 4a + 2 + 3a + 5 = 7a + 7.

    Remember: Unlike terms cannot be combined by addition. For example, 2x + 3y cannot be simplified to 5xy.

  • Linear Equations

    A linear equation is a mathematical statement showing that two expressions are equal. It usually has an unknown variable with a power of 1.

    Example: x + 4 = 10 is a linear equation.

    Solving equations: Find the value of the unknown that makes the equation true. Perform the same operation on both sides to keep the equation balanced.

    Example 1: x + 4 = 10. Subtract 4 from both sides: x = 10 − 4 = 6.

    Example 2: 3x = 15. Divide both sides by 3: x = 5.

    Equations with two steps: For 2x + 3 = 11, subtract 3 to get 2x = 8, then divide by 2. Therefore, x = 4.

    Checking: Substitute the answer into the original equation. For x = 4, 2(4) + 3 = 11, so the equation is true.

    Real-life application: If three identical exercise books cost KSh 150, the cost of one book is 3x = 150, so x = KSh 50.

  • Linear Inequalities

    A linear inequality compares two expressions using symbols instead of an equals sign.

    Symbols: < means less than; > means greater than; ≤ means less than or equal to; ≥ means greater than or equal to.

    Example 1: x + 3 < 8. Subtract 3 from both sides to get x < 5.

    Example 2: 2x ≥ 10. Divide both sides by 2 to get x ≥ 5.

    Number line: Use an open circle for < or >, and a filled circle for ≤ or ≥. Shade the values that satisfy the inequality.

    Compound inequalities: These combine two conditions. For example, 2 < x ≤ 6 means x is greater than 2 and at most 6.

    Remember: If you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, reverse the inequality sign. For example, −2x < 6 gives x > −3.

    Real-life application: If you have KSh 200 and a notebook costs KSh 50, the number n you can buy satisfies 50n ≤ 200, so n ≤ 4.

3. Measurements
  • Pythagorean Relationship

    The Pythagorean relationship applies to a right-angled triangle. It connects the lengths of the three sides.

    Key terms: The hypotenuse is the longest side and lies opposite the right angle. The other two sides are called the shorter sides or legs.

    Rule: For a right-angled triangle, a² + b² = c², where a and b are the shorter sides and c is the hypotenuse.

    Example: If the shorter sides are 3 cm and 4 cm, then c² = 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25. Therefore, c = 5 cm.

    Finding a shorter side: If the hypotenuse is 13 cm and one shorter side is 5 cm, the other side is √(13² − 5²) = √144 = 12 cm.

    Real-life application: This relationship can help calculate a diagonal distance, such as the diagonal across a rectangular field.

    Remember: Use this rule only for right-angled triangles.

  • Length

    Length measures how far apart two points are. Common units include millimetres (mm), centimetres (cm), decimetres (dm), metres (m) and kilometres (km).

    Conversions: 10 mm = 1 cm; 10 cm = 1 dm; 10 dm = 1 m; 1,000 m = 1 km.

    Example: Convert 3.5 m to centimetres. Since 1 m = 100 cm, 3.5 m = 350 cm.

    Addition and subtraction: Convert measurements to the same unit before calculating. For example, 2 m + 50 cm = 200 cm + 50 cm = 250 cm, or 2.5 m.

    Perimeter: The total distance around a shape. For a rectangle, perimeter = 2 × (length + width).

    Example: A rectangle measuring 8 cm by 3 cm has perimeter 2 × (8 + 3) = 22 cm.

    Circumference: The distance around a circle. Use C = πd or C = 2πr, where d is the diameter and r is the radius.

    Example: A circle with diameter 14 cm has circumference approximately 22 × 2 = 44 cm when π is approximated as 22/7.

    Remember: Perimeter and circumference are measured in units of length, such as cm or m, not square units.

  • Area

    Area is the amount of surface covered by a two-dimensional shape. It is measured in square units, such as cm² and m².

    Rectangle: Area = length × width.

    Example: A rectangle 8 m long and 5 m wide has area 8 × 5 = 40 m².

    Parallelogram: Area = base × perpendicular height.

    Rhombus: Area = base × perpendicular height. It can also be found using half the product of its diagonals.

    Trapezium: Area = ½ × (sum of parallel sides) × perpendicular height.

    Circle: Area = πr², where r is the radius. For a circle of radius 7 cm, area = (22/7) × 7 × 7 = 154 cm².

    Unit conversions: 1 m² = 10,000 cm²; 1 hectare = 10,000 m²; 1 acre is approximately 4,047 m².

    Combined shapes: Divide the shape into familiar shapes, calculate their areas, then add or subtract as needed.

    Remember: A border around a shape may require finding the outer area and subtracting the inner area.

  • Volume and Capacity

    Volume is the space occupied by a three-dimensional object. It is measured in cubic units, such as cm³ and m³.

    Capacity is the amount a container can hold, commonly measured in millilitres (mL) and litres (L).

    Cube: Volume = side × side × side = side³.

    Cuboid: Volume = length × width × height.

    Example: A cuboid measuring 5 cm × 3 cm × 2 cm has volume 5 × 3 × 2 = 30 cm³.

    Cylinder: Volume = πr²h, where r is the radius and h is the perpendicular height.

    Conversions: 1 m³ = 1,000,000 cm³; 1 L = 1,000 mL; and 1 cm³ = 1 mL.

    Example: A container holding 2.5 L holds 2,500 mL.

    Remember: Volume describes space occupied, while capacity describes how much a container can hold.

  • Time, Distance and Speed

    Time measures how long an event takes. Common units include seconds, minutes and hours.

    Time conversions: 60 seconds = 1 minute; 60 minutes = 1 hour; 24 hours = 1 day.

    Distance is the length of the path travelled. It may be measured in metres or kilometres.

    Speed is the distance travelled per unit of time.

    Formula: Speed = Distance ÷ Time.

    Finding distance: Distance = Speed × Time.

    Finding time: Time = Distance ÷ Speed.

    Example: A cyclist travels 30 km in 2 hours. Speed = 30 ÷ 2 = 15 km/h.

    Example: A bus travels at 60 km/h for 3 hours. Distance = 60 × 3 = 180 km.

    Remember: Make sure the units agree. For example, use kilometres with hours to obtain speed in km/h.

  • Temperature

    Temperature tells us how hot or cold an object or environment is.

    Celsius scale (°C): Commonly used in Kenya for weather reports and everyday measurements.

    Kelvin scale (K): A temperature scale used in science. Kelvin values do not use the degree symbol.

    Conversion: Temperature in kelvin = temperature in degrees Celsius + 273.15.

    Example: 25°C = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15 K.

    Reverse conversion: Temperature in degrees Celsius = temperature in kelvin − 273.15.

    Example: 300 K = 300 − 273.15 = 26.85°C.

    Important reference points: At standard atmospheric pressure, water freezes at 0°C (273.15 K) and boils at 100°C (373.15 K).

    Remember: Temperature is different from heat. Temperature indicates how hot or cold something is, while heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

  • Money

    Money is used to buy goods and services. In Kenya, the currency is the Kenyan shilling (KSh), divided into 100 cents.

    Addition and subtraction: Align the decimal points when working with amounts that include cents.

    Example: KSh 125.50 + KSh 74.50 = KSh 200.00.

    Multiplication: Multiply the price of one item by the number of items bought.

    Example: Three exercise books at KSh 45 each cost 3 × 45 = KSh 135.

    Change: Change = Amount paid − Total cost.

    Example: If you pay KSh 500 for goods costing KSh 365, your change is 500 − 365 = KSh 135.

    Budgeting: A budget helps plan income and expenses so that spending does not exceed available money.

    Profit: Profit = Selling price − Cost price, when the selling price is greater than the cost price.

    Loss: Loss = Cost price − Selling price, when the cost price is greater than the selling price.

    Remember: Keep money units consistent and check your calculations before giving change.

4. Geometry
  • Angles

    An angle is formed when two rays meet at a common endpoint called the vertex.

    Types of angles: Acute angles are less than 90°; right angles equal 90°; obtuse angles are greater than 90° but less than 180°; straight angles equal 180°.

    Angles on a straight line: Angles on a straight line add up to 180°.

    Example: If one angle on a straight line is 65°, the other is 180° − 65° = 115°.

    Angles around a point: Angles around a point add up to 360°.

    Vertically opposite angles: When two straight lines cross, opposite angles are equal.

    Parallel lines: When a transversal crosses parallel lines, corresponding angles are equal and alternate interior angles are equal. Co-interior angles on the same side of the transversal add up to 180°.

    Polygons: A polygon is a closed shape with straight sides. The sum of interior angles of an n-sided polygon is (n − 2) × 180°.

    Example: The sum of interior angles of a hexagon is (6 − 2) × 180° = 720°.

    Remember: Use a protractor to measure angles and include the degree symbol (°) in your answers.

  • Geometrical Constructions

    Geometrical construction is the accurate drawing of shapes and angles using mathematical instruments, especially a ruler and a pair of compasses.

    Instruments: Use a ruler to draw straight lines, compasses to draw arcs and circles, and a protractor when measuring angles.

    Angle bisector: A line that divides an angle into two equal angles. For example, bisecting 60° gives two angles of 30° each.

    Common angles: Practise constructing 90°, 60°, 45° and 30° using suitable compass-and-ruler methods.

    Constructing 90°: A perpendicular line forms a right angle of 90° with another line.

    Constructing 45°: Bisect a 90° angle to obtain two angles of 45°.

    Constructing 30°: Bisect a 60° angle to obtain two angles of 30°.

    Triangles: Draw a triangle accurately using the given side lengths or angle and side information, where the measurements determine a unique triangle.

    Circles: Use a compass to draw a circle with a specified radius. The compass opening represents the radius.

    Good practice: Keep construction arcs visible until the construction is complete, and label points and angles clearly.

5. Data Handling and Probability
  • Data Handling

    Data are facts or information collected for a particular purpose. Data handling involves collecting, organising, presenting and interpreting data.

    Collecting data: Information can be collected through questionnaires, interviews, observations, measurements or counting.

    Organising data: Arrange data in a table or frequency table to make it easier to understand.

    Frequency: The number of times a value occurs in a data set.

    Example: For the scores 2, 3, 2, 4, 3, 2, the frequency of 2 is 3, the frequency of 3 is 2, and the frequency of 4 is 1.

    Presenting data: Data may be displayed using tables, pictograms, bar graphs or other suitable charts.

    Interpreting data: Read tables and graphs to identify information, compare values and draw conclusions supported by the data.

    Mean: Add all the values and divide by the number of values.

    Example: The mean of 2, 4 and 6 is (2 + 4 + 6) ÷ 3 = 4.

    Probability: Probability describes how likely an event is to happen. It ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).

    Simple probability: When outcomes are equally likely, probability = number of favourable outcomes ÷ total number of possible outcomes.

    Example: A fair coin has two equally likely outcomes. The probability of getting heads is 1/2.

    Remember: Use the information provided to support your conclusions; do not assume something the data does not show.
